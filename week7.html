Professional Etiquette in Communication

This week focused on understanding the importance of professional etiquette in communication. We learned how to communicate politely and effectively through telephone calls, emails and social media. The session also explained how proper behaviour, respectful language and good communication habits help us create a positive impression when interacting with lecturers, employers and colleagues in both academic and professional environments.

Telephone etiquette

TopicGuidance
The basicsSpeak directly into the mouthpiece, don't eat or chew gum, don't cover the phone with your hand, and use the hold button. Always be courteous.
AnsweringPick up within 2 to 3 rings and greet clearly, for example “Thank you for calling (department). How may I help you?”
HoldingAsk permission first, check back every 30 to 45 seconds, and thank the caller for waiting.
TransferringExplain why, get the caller's agreement, confirm the other person can take the call, and tell the caller who they are being transferred to.
Taking messagesRecord who it's for, the caller's name and number, the date and time, the message and the action needed. Deliver it quickly and keep it confidential.
Outbound callsCheck the number, plan your objectives, take notes and state any follow-up action.
Ending a callUse a closing phrase, state the next steps, thank the caller, and avoid slang such as “bye-bye” or “okie-dokie”.

Professional telephone language

AvoidSay instead
“Hold on.”“May I put you on hold?”
“Who's calling?”“May I say who is calling, please?”
“I can't hear you, speak up!”“I am having a little difficulty hearing you. Can you please speak up?”
“I can't help you. Speak to someone else.”“I need to transfer your call so they can answer your question. May I do so?”
“Your problem” or “your complaint”“Your question” or “your concern”

Email etiquette: seven rules

RuleWhy
1. Include a subject and use the recipient's name and correct titleIt tells the reader what the email is about and shows respect
2. Don't write in ALL CAPITALSIt reads as shouting
3. Don't send confidential informationMedical, ID and bank details could be stolen if the account is accessed
4. Take care with abbreviations and emoticons“LOL” or “IDK” are not professional and may not be understood
5. Think before you sendAsk whether it is relevant, whether it could offend or be misread, and whether it could cause trouble
6. Use correct grammar and proofreadErrors can cost you a job or a role
7. Don't forward unnecessary emailsForward only what is valuable, with a personal comment

Remember: every email you send leaves a permanent copy with each recipient.

Social media

Employers and universities often look at applicants' accounts. Use social media to build your brand, and be careful about what you post, because inappropriate posts can cost you an offer or even a job.

What I Learned

Through this session, I learned that professional communication is not only about what we say, but also about how we express ourselves. Small details such as using polite language, giving proper greetings and checking emails before sending them can create a positive impression and build trust with others. I realised that I had not always paid much attention to these simple habits, especially when communicating through phone calls and emails. This session helped me understand how important they are in maintaining professionalism. Moving forward, I want to apply these practices when communicating with lecturers, employers and colleagues. I also plan to be more mindful of what I share on social media, ensuring that my online presence reflects the positive personal brand I want to build.
